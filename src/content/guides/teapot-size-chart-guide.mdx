## Why "Teapot Cups" Never Match Modern Mugs (The 6 oz Rule)

Most sizing disappointment starts with one misleading word on product boxes: **"cup."** In tea hardware, "cup" carries three wildly different definitions depending on geography and era.

### Traditional 6 oz Teacup vs. 8 oz Measuring Cup vs. 12 oz Mug

When heritage European potteries or cast-iron foundries stamp "4-Cup" or "6-Cup" on a box, they are measuring against the classic **5 to 6 oz (150ml to 180ml) porcelain teacup** sitting on a saucer—not a standard **8 oz (237ml) US baking measuring cup**, and certainly not the **11 to 12 oz (325ml to 355ml) stoneware mugs** stocked in modern kitchens.

Do the quick arithmetic and the gap becomes obvious:

* **Three classic 6 oz teacups** require roughly **540ml** of poured liquor.
* **Three modern 11 oz coffee-style mugs** require **990ml** of poured liquor—nearly double the teapot capacity!

If you sit at your desk drinking out of a tall ceramic mug holding 330ml, buying a "2-cup" 350ml pot means one steep fills your mug once to the collar with zero warm second pour waiting behind it. Conversely, buying a 1,000ml "6-cup" teapot for morning solo tea guarantees half the pot sits cooling on the table unless you drink three large mugs in one sitting.

### The 15% Usable Yield Rule: Headspace, Spout Level & Leaf Displacement

Manufacturer specs almost always report **brim capacity**—meaning water poured right up to the upper lip with the lid removed and zero leaves inside. In daily kitchen practice, three physical factors shave **10% to 15%** off that headline number:

1. **Lid Seating & Spout Collar Clearance:** Filling a pot to the absolute brim splashes boiling water across the rim when you drop the lid in and forces tea prematurely up the spout throat before you even lift the handle. Safe working fill stops 10mm to 15mm below the upper gallery.
2. **Wet Leaf Water Absorption:** Loose tea acts like an organic sponge. Ten grams of rolled high-mountain oolong easily absorbs **20ml to 25ml** of trapped liquid that remains inside the spent leaves after pouring.
3. **Basket & Infuser Displacement:** Thick stainless steel baskets and whole expanded tea foliage displace physical volume inside smaller pots.

In real testing across our studio bench, a 450ml vessel consistently yields **385ml to 400ml** of clean cup liquor, while an 800ml sharing pot yields roughly **680ml to 710ml**. ## Choosing Capacity by Brewing Style (Gongfu, Kyusu & Western)

Rather than shopping purely by total ounces, anchor your teapot volume to **how many infusions** you want from each scoop of leaves.

### Solo Gongfu & Japanese Kyusu (150ml – 320ml)

East Asian tea rituals invert Western proportions: instead of loading 3 grams of leaves into 700ml of water once, you pack **5 to 8 grams** of whole leaves into a tiny chamber and flash-steep repeatedly across five to ten rounds.

If you tried Gongfu brewing inside a 600ml Western teapot, two things would happen fast: you would burn through 25 grams of expensive artisanal oolong in one afternoon, and you would drink nearly four liters of tea before infusion six! ## Infuser Basket Depth & The Danger of Underfilling Large Pots

Many first-time buyers assume buying a bigger teapot is "safer" because you can always fill a 1,000ml pot halfway when drinking alone. In reality, **underfilling a large teapot is one of the fastest ways to brew weak, lukewarm tea**.

### Why Steep Time Collapses When a Basket Hangs High

Look inside a standard removable basket teapot before buying. In many decorative 900ml or 1,200ml Western teapots, the stainless steel mesh basket hangs only halfway down the belly—stopping 5cm to 7cm above the ceramic floor.

Pour 300ml of boiling water into that 1,000ml pot for a solo mug, and the water line barely kisses the bottom millimeter of the metal strainer. Ninety percent of your dry leaves sit stranded above the water in steam! Unless a high-volume pot uses a **floor-skimming extra-deep basket**—like the deep 18-8 cylinder engineered into the [Hario Chacha Kyusu Maru (700ml)](/gallery/hario-chacha-kyusu-maru-700/), which reaches within millimeters of the rounded glass base—underfilling by half ruins leaf saturation completely.

### Thermal Loss in Half-Empty Teapots

Even if you steep loose leaves free-floating on the bottom without a hanging basket, a half-filled teapot loses extraction heat dramatically faster than a full one. Why? Because rising steam immediately dumps thermal energy into a large pocket of cooler air trapped beneath the lid, accelerating convective cooling along tall exposed side walls.

If you regularly alternate between solo Tuesday mugs and Saturday four-person gatherings, **own two properly sized pots** rather than trying to force one giant 1.2-liter pot to pull solo duty. Keeping a compact 320ml–450ml vessel beside a 950ml–1,200ml hosting pot yields measurably sweeter solo steeps and effortless table refills—and keeps routine rinsing fast as covered in our [complete teapot care guide](/guides/care-maintenance/complete-teapot-care-guide/).

## Quick Sizing Checklist Before You Buy

Before finalizing capacity on your next vessel, run through this quick three-step kitchen verification so volume matches your actual cups and wrists:

### 1. Measure Your Actual Daily Mug First

Do not guess how many ounces your favorite stoneware mug holds—many handmade mugs marketed as "12 oz" actually hold 14 oz to the rim, while delicate vintage teacups hold barely 5 oz. Set your everyday mug on a digital kitchen scale, zero it out, and fill it with tap water to your normal sipping height:

* **150g – 200g water:** Your serving is 5 to 7 oz -> a **320ml** pot serves two cups cleanly.
* **280g – 350g water:** Your serving is 10 to 12 oz -> a **450ml** pot hits solo perfection, or **700ml** for two.
* **Multiplying by 3–4 guests:** Multiply your measured cup weight by guest count, then add **15%** for leaf absorption and safe spout headspace before reading our **teapot size chart ml to cups** above.

### 2. Match Pot Weight & Wrist Ergonomics to Full Water Load

One liter of water weighs exactly **1 kilogram (2.2 pounds)** before counting the dry weight of the pot itself. Add 1,000ml of boiling water to a heavy sand-cast iron pot, and you are lifting over **2.5 kilograms (5.5+ pounds)** on one extended wrist!

* Above **800ml**, strongly prefer a generous **C-handle or balanced top arch handle** that supports multi-finger grip rather than a dainty two-finger teacup ear or long cantilevered side handle.
* For **side-handle (*yokode*) kyusu** vessels, keep volume between **220ml and 400ml** so natural forearm rotation stays effortless and wrist strain stays zero.
