## Borosilicate 3.3 vs. Soda-Lime Glass: Why Chemistry Dictates Safety

Not all clear glass behaves the same way when you pour 208°F (98°C) water into a chilled vessel at room temperature. Standard household tumblers and decorative pitchers are typically molded from **soda-lime glass**, which carries a high coefficient of thermal expansion (roughly 9 × 10⁻⁶ /K). When boiling water heats the inner wall faster than heat conducts to the outer face, internal tensile stress builds rapidly—and the vessel snaps along the weakest seam, usually where the handle or spout meets the belly.

A true **borosilicate glass teapot** uses silica infused with roughly 12% to 15% boron trioxide. That formulation slashes thermal expansion down to roughly 3.3 × 10⁻⁶ /K—meaning the glass wall barely shifts dimensionally across a 300°F (150°C) temperature swing.

Even within authentic borosilicate teapots, build thickness and flameworking matter enormously:

* **Uniform Wall Distribution:** Hand-blown **flowering teapot** bodies should maintain consistent 2.5mm to 3.5mm wall thickness across the lower curve and base. Paper-thin blown ornaments cool faster and shatter easily against sink edges.
* **Annealed Spout & Handle Joins:** Look closely at where the spout root attaches to the round chamber. Smooth, rounded glass fillets indicate proper kiln annealing to relieve internal stress, whereas sharp creases trap tension.
* **Optical Clarity Without Ripples:** Look straight through the center belly toward text on paper. High-grade borosilicate exhibits crisp refraction with minimal wave distortion, ensuring blooming tea bulbs remain sharp and jewel-like from every seat at the table.

## Can You Put a Glass Teapot on a Stove? (Thermal Rules Explained)

One of the top questions readers ask us is **"can you put glass teapot on stove"** burners—and the short answer is *only when both the glass specification and heat source align*.

Many everyday glass tea servers, including the [Hario ChaCha Kyusu Maru 700ml](/gallery/hario-chacha-kyusu-maru-700/), are engineered strictly as **infusion vessels** designed to receive pre-boiled water from a dedicated kettle. Placing a standard pouring vessel directly onto a blazing stove coil concentrates radiant heat onto a tiny ring, starving the side walls of uniform expansion and risking catastrophic failure.

Even when using an explicitly stovetop-rated **borosilicate glass teapot** such as the [Teabloom Celebration Borosilicate Flowering Teapot](/gallery/teabloom-celebration-stovetop-glass/), follow five non-negotiable stovetop safety rules:

1. **Always Fill Water Before Ignition:** Never pre-heat an empty glass teapot on a burner. Fill the chamber at least one-third full with room-temperature or warm water before turning on heat.
2. **Respect Stove Type Differences:**
   * **Gas Ranges:** Keep the flame low and strictly smaller than the flat base footprint. Use a metal heat-diffuser trivet whenever possible so flame tongues never lick the curved glass sidewall or handle root.
   * **Radiant Electric & Glass Ceramic Tops:** Use low to medium heat only. Exposed glowing coil burners deliver intense pinpoint heat—keep contact brief and lift by a dry potholder if needed.
   * **Induction Cooktops:** Glass is non-magnetic and will **never heat** on an induction stove unless the pot sits atop a ferromagnetic stainless induction diffuser disk.
3. **Avoid Rapid Thermal Reversal:** Never lift a hot stovetop glass pot directly off a burner and set it onto cold marble, wet granite, or ice. Rest it on a cork trivet, bamboo mat, or wooden tray.
4. **Watch the Spout Water Level:** Boiling creates vigorous convection bubbles. Fill only to the maximum recommended shoulder line so boiling surges do not blast scalding liquid out the spout.
5. **Prefer Candle Tea-Light Warmers for Serving:** Once your tea reaches infusion temperature, moving the vessel onto a vented glass or ceramic tea-light warmer preserves gentle drinking warmth without re-boiling delicate leaves into bitterness.

## Blooming Tea Vessel Geometry: Why Belly Shape Changes Everything

A dedicated **blooming tea teapot** has very different spatial requirements from an everyday **loose leaf teapot**. A single hand-tied tea blossom typically measures 25mm dry, yet unfurls vertically into an 80mm to 100mm floral arch crowned with globe amaranth, jasmine strands, or calendula petals.

If you drop a flowering tea bulb into a narrow 600ml glass carafe or leave a metal infuser basket hanging inside the lid, two things go wrong immediately: the compressed petals fold awkwardly against the sidewalls, and stagnant water beneath the lid fails to circulate cleanly around the core leaves.

When shopping specifically for a **flowering teapot**, check three spatial benchmarks:

* **Internal Belly Diameter (115mm+):** A broad, low-slung sphere or flattened dome creates natural lateral stage room without magnifying lens distortion.
* **Vertical Headroom (95mm–120mm):** Tall blossoms need room to stretch cleanly beneath the lid collar without brushing against glass stops.
* **Removable Infuser Hardware:** Choose a model where the glass or metal infuser lifts out entirely, leaving a completely unobstructed optical chamber for blooming bulbs—then snaps right back in when you switch to daily loose-leaf jasmine or oolong.
